Part II: Topics in ML

Sparse Coding and Matching Pursuit

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the sparse coding problem: a measurement explained by a few items from a large known collection
  • Explain why least squares on the whole collection fails when unknowns outnumber measurements
  • Recover two overlapping genes from one pixel by hand, by peeling off the strongest match first
  • Show why one-at-a-time peeling gets the amounts wrong when the pieces overlap
  • State the one-line fix that turns matching pursuit into orthogonal matching pursuit

Motivation

  • In Part I, every prediction was a sum of simple blocks, and training shaped the blocks. This chapter flips the problem: the blocks are handed to us, fixed, in a big labelled box, and the question becomes which few of them are present, and in what amounts.
  • That question has a name, sparse coding, and it appears whenever a measurement is a mixture of a small number of known signatures: which instruments are playing in this chord, which materials are in this spectrum, which genes are in this pixel.
  • We will use the gene version, because it is real: this chapter's algorithm, run once per pixel, is how real spatial gene-expression pipelines decode their images.
  • The plan: solve an easy pixel by eye, peel apart a hard one by hand, watch chapter 1's least squares fail at the same task, and then meet the two algorithms we will have accidentally invented along the way.

1. Every Gene Gets a Barcode

  • The experiment, in two sentences: each gene's mRNA molecules are tagged in the tissue, and the same tissue is photographed over several rounds, in several colour channels per round. The tags are designed so that each gene lights up in its own sequence of colours: one colour per round.
  • So each gene has a barcode: a small rounds × channels grid showing which channel lights up in each round. Our codebook uses 3 rounds and 4 channels: in each round (column), exactly one channel (row) lights up.
  • A confession about the word: strictly, the barcode is the gene's dye sequence (Plp1's below is dye 1 → dye 2 → dye 3), and the grid is that sequence as the camera sees it, each channel being tuned to one dye. We use "barcode" for the grid throughout, because the grid is the form all the math touches. Real dyes also spill a little into neighbouring channels; ignored here.
  • Here is the full codebook for a made-up panel of 16 genes, each lit cell painted in its dye's colour, so every barcode reads as a colour sequence (hover any cell):
  • Two things to notice before moving on:
    • A barcode is 12 numbers (3 × 4), but underneath it is only 3 choices of dye, so with 4 dyes and 3 rounds there are just $4^3 = 64$ possible barcodes. With 16 genes (real panels use hundreds), barcodes are forced to share dyes in some rounds. Plp1 and Sst above agree in rounds 1 and 3 and differ only in round 2. Keep those two in mind.
    • A pixel of the photographed tissue is also 12 numbers: its brightness in each round and channel. If one gene's molecule sits at that pixel, the pixel should look like that gene's barcode, scaled by how bright the tag is.
  • The model, then, is chapter 1's weighted sum with the blocks fixed: $\;\text{pixel} \approx \beta_1\,\text{barcode}_{g_1} + \beta_2\,\text{barcode}_{g_2} + \cdots\;$ where only a few $\beta$'s should be nonzero, and none should be negative (a gene cannot glow by a negative amount).
  • The task for the rest of the chapter: given a pixel's 12 numbers, recover which genes and how bright.

2. The Easy Pixel

  • Here is a pixel from our imaginary tissue, drawn with the same colour code as the codebook: each row keeps its channel's dye colour, and the depth of the colour is the measured brightness. The camera measures amounts of light, so a pixel is a grid of 12 brightnesses. Your task: find its gene by eye. Scroll between the pixel and the codebook above until you spot the matching barcode.
  • Then press the button, which does the same thing with arithmetic: for every gene it computes a looks-like score, measuring how well the pixel's pattern matches that barcode (1 = same pattern, 0 = nothing in common).
  • One bar towers and the rest are noise: this pixel is Gad1, at brightness about 5. The runner-up scores around 0.35, and only because it happens to share a dye with Gad1 in one round.
  • If every pixel looked like this, the chapter would end here: compare against each barcode, keep the best match. No fitting, no training, done.

What did the button just compute?

  • The score is simpler than it sounds. Pick any gene below and follow three steps:
    1. Lay that gene's barcode over the pixel. Its 3 lit cells are outlined.
    2. Add up the pixel's brightness inside the outlines. The matching gene's outlines sit exactly on the pixel's bright cells and collect everything; a wrong gene's outlines land mostly on dark cells and collect almost nothing. (Try Cck: it shares one dye with Gad1, so it collects exactly one bright cell.)
    3. Divide by the two lengths (the pixel's overall brightness and the barcode's size), so a bright pixel does not out-score a dim one just by being bright. The result lives between $-1$ and $1$, and it only reaches $1$ when the pixel is the barcode times a single brightness.
  • In symbols, with the sums running over all 12 cells: $\text{score} = \dfrac{\sum \text{pixel} \times \text{barcode}} {\sqrt{\sum \text{pixel}^2}\sqrt{\sum \text{barcode}^2}}$. This has a standard name: the cosine similarity of the two grids. Because our barcode cells are 0 or 1, the top is literally "add up the pixel where the barcode glows".
  • If it reminds you of the Pearson correlation, it should: Pearson is this same formula applied after subtracting each grid's mean. Neither is better in general; they answer different questions:

  • Here, cosine is the right one, for two reasons. First, our zero is physical: zero means "no light", and our model says a one-gene pixel is a scaled copy of its barcode, which is exactly the question cosine asks. Second, cosine is the score that matches least squares: after section 5's re-fit, the leftover's cosine with every picked gene is exactly zero, and that zero is the "orthogonal" in the algorithm's name.
  • Pearson would be the wrong one here, not just an equivalent alternative. Its mean-subtraction shifts the barcode's 0s and 1s, and a perfectly explained pixel would still score about $-0.19$ against its own picked genes instead of zero: a correct answer that looks unfinished. Save Pearson for the questions it was built for, where each variable's baseline is arbitrary and only the co-movement carries information.
  • So, to be exact: every bar plot on this page, and the selection score inside matching pursuit and OMP, is cosine similarity, not Pearson correlation. (People often say "correlation" loosely for both; when this chapter says score, it means cosine.) It is the engine of the whole chapter: the algorithm we are building picks genes greedily by this score, first on the pixel, then on each leftover.

3. The Hard Pixel: Peel It Apart

  • Tissue is crowded, and two molecules from different genes often land on the same pixel. Then the pixel is a sum of two barcodes at different brightnesses, and it matches nothing in the codebook exactly.
  • To be clear about what "mixed" means: both genes are physically there. Two molecules, two glows, one camera cell adding up their light. The split we are about to recover is an accounting of that light ("6 units of Plp1 plus 2.5 of Sst"), not a list of alternative guesses for one uncertain molecule. A pixel that is merely noisy or blurry does not earn a second gene: it gets its one gene plus a leftover that looks like noise, and the right response to noise is to stop, not to add genes.
  • Here is such a pixel, in a panel with three grids:
    • pixel colours: the measurement, fixed;
    • pick 1 + pick 2: the weighted sum of your two picks below, $\beta_1 \times \text{barcode}_{\text{pick 1}} + \beta_2 \times \text{barcode}_{\text{pick 2}}$;
    • the leftover: the pixel minus your picks' sum, whatever your picks have not accounted for yet. (In regression language, the residual.)
  • The looks-like scores at the bottom are live, and they score the leftover, not the pixel: as your picks improve, the bars tell you what is left to find.
  • Your task: explain this pixel. A good strategy, in three steps:
    1. The tallest bar is your first suspect. Select it as pick 1 and raise its brightness until the leftover stops glowing in that gene's cells. If cells turn blue, you have removed brightness that was never there: back off. (Or press best brightness and let least squares set the slider.)
    2. Now look at the leftover and the bars again. Gray and flat? You are done. Still glowing in a pattern? Score it: a second gene, invisible a moment ago behind the first one, now tops the bars.
    3. Add it as pick 2, set its brightness, and drive the leftover down to noise.
  • There is also a camera noise slider. At 0 the camera is perfect and your peel can drive the leftover to exactly zero. Push it up and watch the looks-like bars blur: past some point the dim gene's bar sinks among the noise bars, and no method, greedy or otherwise, could tell it from chance.

  • You just recovered a gene that was invisible in the raw pixel: nothing in the codebook matches the mixed pixel, but once the dominant gene is peeled off, the second one is unmistakable in the leftover.
  • This strategy, find the best match, subtract it, repeat on the leftover, is a real algorithm called matching pursuit. You have been running it by hand.
  • But why all this peeling? We know how to fit a weighted sum of known pieces to data. That is least squares, from the very first section of these notes. Why not just fit all 16 genes at once and read off which $\beta$'s are big?

4. Why Not Least Squares on Every Gene?

  • Let's try it. One press fits $\;\text{pixel} \approx \beta_1\,\text{barcode}_1 + \cdots + \beta_{16}\,\text{barcode}_{16}\;$ to the same hard pixel (at its default camera noise), choosing all 16 $\beta$'s to minimize the MSE, with no other constraint. The truth (6 of Plp1, 2.5 of Sst, nothing else) is drawn as diamonds.
MSE:  (the best fit on this page, and the most wrong answer)
  • The fit is nearly perfect, and the answer is nonsense: every gene is reported present, several with sizeable brightness, some negative. A pixel does not contain sixteen genes, and no gene glows by $-0.8$.
  • The failure is structural, not numerical. The pixel is 12 numbers; we asked for 16 unknowns. With more unknowns than measurements there are infinitely many settings of the $\beta$'s with essentially zero error, and least squares has no reason to prefer the physical one. It smears the pixel's brightness across the whole codebook. (Real panels are worse: hundreds of genes against a few dozen numbers per pixel.)
  • What least squares is missing is what you did naturally in section 3: decide who is present before fitting amounts. Selection first, regression after, and regression only ever on the short list of picks. The fitting was never the problem; the guest list was.
  • "Why not the lasso, then?" A fair question if you have met regularization. The penalty we would really want is the count of genes used (the so-called L0 penalty), but minimizing with a count means trying subsets, the combinatorial search we are avoiding. The lasso swaps the count for $\lambda \sum_g |\beta_g|$ (L1), which is solvable and really does set many $\beta$'s to exactly zero. It can work here; the reasons this chapter (and the real pipeline) goes greedy instead are practical. The lasso needs $\lambda$ tuned until "about one to three genes per pixel" comes out, and the same penalty that zeroes the losers also shrinks the winners, biasing the brightness estimates low. The standard repair is to re-fit the selected genes by plain least squares, which is precisely the re-fit step of the algorithm in the next section. Greedy selection just gets there directly, a few cheap steps per pixel.
  • "Then why not learn the solver?" One more step down the lasso road, and it lands back in Part I. The lasso is solved by repeating two moves, hundreds of times over: a small least squares step, then shrink-the-coefficients-and-zero-the-small-ones. Now write out just ten of those repeats, one after another, and look at what each one does: a weighted sum of its inputs, followed by a simple nonlinearity. That is a layer. Ten repeats written out make a ten-layer network (in the literature this trick is called unrolling the solver, and networks built this way are unrolled algorithms), and the shrink step is just a shifted ReLU, so the whole thing is built from chapter 1's lego blocks. Let the weights be learned from example pixels instead of copied from the codebook, and you get LISTA (learned ISTA): a network that produces sparse codes in one fast pass. Its output is the same kind of answer as everything else in this chapter (brightnesses per gene, most of them zero), not probabilities, and not alternative guesses for an uncertain pixel. It fixes both complaints above, the slow iterations and the shrinkage bias, but it pays with everything training always costs: it needs examples with trustworthy answers to imitate, it only holds for data that looks like its training data, and a new codebook means training again. For a pipeline that must work on any tissue with any gene panel out of the box, the plain greedy loop keeps winning on simplicity, which is why it is the one we now build.

5. The Flaw in Peeling, and the One-Line Fix

  • So peeling wins. But it has a subtle flaw, and you may have already met it: with both genes picked and both best brightness buttons pressed once, the leftover in section 3 still glowed a little. Why?
  • Recall that Plp1 and Sst share dyes in rounds 1 and 3. When we set Plp1's brightness first, alone, least squares gave it credit for everything in the shared cells, including brightness that actually belongs to Sst. Peeling one at a time made Plp1 steal from Sst:

  • One-at-a-time peeling lands on $\beta \approx 7.8$ and $1.4$; the truth is $6$ and $2.5$. The overshoot on Plp1 is exactly the part of Sst that lives in their shared cells, and it then goes missing from Sst's own estimate.
  • The fix costs one line: after every new pick, re-fit all picked genes together with least squares. Two picks, two unknowns, twelve numbers: well determined, and the shared cells are split correctly because both genes are on the guest list at once. The estimates snap to $6.2$ and $2.5$, and the leftover drops to pure noise.
  • Peeling with this re-fit is called orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP): "orthogonal" because after the re-fit, the leftover has nothing in common with any picked barcode: zero looks-like score, nothing left to steal, and no gene ever gets picked twice.
  • To be precise about what the re-fit fixes: it fixes the amounts. Given the picked genes, joint least squares splits the shared cells correctly. It does not fix a wrong pick: had the tallest bar pointed at the wrong gene, the re-fit would only tidy the amounts of a wrong guest list. Both algorithms choose greedily and neither can un-pick; the protections are the stopping rule below, and a codebook designed so that barcodes do not overlap too much.
  • If you kept alternating the two best-brightness buttons in section 3 forever, the sliders would crawl to these same values: the re-fit just jumps there in one solve.
  • When does the algorithm stop picking? When the leftover looks like noise: no bar stands out, and taking more genes would only fit the noise, chapter 5's overfitting in miniature.
  • So the full recipe is: score, pick the best match, re-fit the picks together, repeat on the leftover, stop at noise. Selection is greedy, amounts come from a tiny least squares, and the codebook-wide fit of section 4 is never needed.

(Optional) Both Algorithms in Code

  • Here is the hand-peeling of section 3 as a numpy loop. This is matching pursuit: fit the newest pick alone against the current leftover, subtract it, never look back.
def mp(pixel, codebook, n_picks):          # codebook: (12, 16), one column per gene
    picked, beta = [], []
    leftover = pixel.copy()
    for _ in range(n_picks):
        scores = codebook.T @ leftover     # which barcode does the leftover look like?
        g = int(np.abs(scores).argmax())
        picked.append(g)
        code = codebook[:, g]
        b = (code @ leftover) / (code @ code)  # brightness of the NEW pick alone
        beta.append(b)                         # earlier brightnesses never revisited
        leftover = leftover - b * code         # subtract just the new pick
    return picked, beta
  • One detail: scores here are plain sums of products, without section 2's two divisions. That is safe because the divisions cannot change which gene wins: the leftover's length scales every gene's score equally, and all our barcodes have the same length (three lit cells each). The code keeps only what affects the answer.
  • Orthogonal matching pursuit keeps the selection step and changes only the fitting, the three numbered lines below: every picked gene is re-fitted together against the original pixel, and the leftover is rebuilt from the pixel instead of updated in place.
def omp(pixel, codebook, n_picks):
    picked = []
    leftover = pixel.copy()
    for _ in range(n_picks):
        scores = codebook.T @ leftover     # selection: same as mp()
        picked.append(int(np.abs(scores).argmax()))
        chosen = codebook[:, picked]                          # (1) ALL picks so far
        beta = np.linalg.lstsq(chosen, pixel, rcond=None)[0]  # (2) re-fit them together
        leftover = pixel - chosen @ beta                      # (3) rebuilt from the pixel
    return picked, beta
  • That is the entire difference. mp() asks "how bright is my newest pick, given the leftover I happen to have?"; omp() asks "what brightnesses best explain the original pixel, using everything picked so far?". On our hard pixel both pick Plp1 then Sst, but mp() returns brightnesses 7.8 and 1.4 while omp() returns 6.2 and 2.5, the numbers from section 5.

Chapter Highlights

  1. Sparse coding: a measurement is a weighted sum of a few pieces from a large known collection; the job is to find which pieces and their weights.
  2. Least squares on the whole collection fails when unknowns outnumber measurements: it fits perfectly and answers nothing.
  3. Matching pursuit: score every piece against the leftover, pick the best match, subtract, repeat. Selection first, fitting after.
  4. One-at-a-time fitting lets overlapping pieces steal brightness from each other.
  5. Orthogonal matching pursuit re-fits all picked pieces together after every pick, which splits shared cells correctly and leaves a leftover with nothing in common with any pick.
